multimodal wayfinding for smart pitstops

overview

On The Way is a wayfinding product I designed and built end to end: research, requirements, interaction design, visual identity, and a working Angular prototype on the Google Maps API. The core job is simple and high-stakes. Drivers need to get from point A to point B and add up to three stops without leaving the route, losing the destination, or making unsafe decisions behind the wheel. Users can search for a stop or pick from six nearby categories, then get guidance that keeps the main trip intact.

The problem started as a lived user pain. On a family trip to the Outer Banks, we used one phone for hotel directions and another to find a stop. Existing GPS tools kept recommending places behind us, which would have meant turning around and adding time we did not have. I framed that as a design problem: recommend only what lies ahead, and keep every stop close to the primary route. When my supervisor later asked me to demonstrate UX/UI skill by shipping a maps-based Angular app, I used that brief to take the idea through a full product process instead of jumping straight to screens.

final design

The final experience is organized around one principle: discover what lies ahead without sacrificing the trip you already started. Drivers enter a destination, choose a route, add up to three stops by search or category, save favorite places, and end a stop without killing navigation to the hotel, campus, or meeting. I designed the UI for glanceability and low cognitive load because this is a driving context, not a desk task. The current build is a functional prototype, not a production-complete app, and I treat that constraint as part of the case: I prioritized the flows that proved the concept over polishing every edge case.

user research

I started with competitive analysis of Apple Maps, Google Maps, and Waze to find where market-leading GPS products still fail drivers. Apple Maps and Waze only allow one added stop. Apple Maps also blocks address search when adding a stop, limiting people to categories like cafés, gas, and banks. Across all three, “end navigation” ends the entire trip instead of closing just the detour. Those findings became design criteria, not just observations: multiple stops, flexible search, route-aware recommendations, and a clear way to drop a stop and resume the main route.

To keep the product from being designed only around my own trip, I wrote contextual use scenarios and built three personas: a student heading to campus, a family on a road trip, and a professional driving to a meeting. That work surfaced shared needs and different constraints, which I synthesized into a requirements set: generate A-to-B directions, add multiple stops, search by address, keep stops close to the main route, and end navigation without ambiguity. Those requirements were the contract I designed and built against.

ideation and conceptual design

I prototyped the primary task flow against the Erin Wyman persona so every screen had a reason to exist. The scenario walks her from an entered address (not “current location”) to her house in Blacksburg, saving that address to favorites, adding a Shaky’s less than 25 miles from the route, then adding a gas stop. Designing to a specific task kept the prototype honest: if a control did not help Erin complete that trip, it did not belong in the first iteration.

Implementation on the Google Maps API forced tradeoffs. The shipped interaction model differs slightly from the earlier prototype where the API could not support the original behavior, and I redesigned those moments instead of pretending the constraint was not there. In parallel I created the logo and style guide so the product had a coherent visual system. I explored a shark mark because sharks cannot swim backward, which matched “discover what lies ahead,” then cut it when it fought the UI. The final identity is more direct, which is the same judgment I use on product work: a clever idea only stays if it still serves the user.

reflection

On The Way was my first time owning a product across research, design, and implementation. It was also my first time creating a style guide, building an Angular application, and integrating the Google Maps API. I turned a messy, real-world frustration into a problem statement, justified requirements via competitive analysis and use case scenarios, prototyped a critical user flow, and adapted the design when engineering constraints showed up. What I keep from that work is a way of practicing UX: stay with the original frustration long enough to design the flow that would have helped on that drive, then treat every constraint as a reason to redesign, not a reason to drop the user.
