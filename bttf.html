promoting music sharing and artist discovery on spotify

overview

Back to the Future is a Spotify concept I led as designer, working with one partner, for the 2021 D&AD New Blood Awards. The brief asked us to help people share the audio that means the most to them and to raise engagement on social feeds. I treated that as a product problem, not a campaign: listening already happens in Spotify, but sharing still leaks out to other apps. The concept answers with three features—Boombox, Best Buds, and Anthems—that turn a private library into a social experience through public stories, private listening, and a profile that can actually describe your taste.

I owned the end-to-end UX process: framing the brief, running a survey, synthesizing requirements and success metrics, ideating against real sharing behaviors, and prototyping the system in Adobe XD. The through-line was Spotify’s own belief that listening is everything. We designed for connecting people in the present through artifacts of the past: a playlist you are in right now, a session you can join, a song you are willing to pin to your name.

final design

The final system is not one button for “share.” It is three complementary surfaces, each mapped to a different social job. Boombox is a temporary, curated glimpse into what you are playing, so sharing feels like a story instead of a dump of your whole library. Best Buds is a live portal into a mutual follower’s session: you can open the mode so friends can join you, or drop in on someone else. Anthems lets people pin songs to a Spotify profile so taste is visible without a bio paragraph. Together they move Spotify from a streaming utility toward a social product, while keeping each feature scoped to a single, learnable behavior.

user research

Before sketching features, I wrote a Google Forms survey and collected responses from 82 college-aged Spotify users. Two findings set the design direction. 85% were at least somewhat interested in knowing what music their friends are listening to right now, and 67% already received song suggestions outside of Spotify. That gap was the insight: the desire to share and discover already exists, but the product is not capturing it. The opportunity was to pull those recommendations back in-app, between friends and followers, instead of inventing a new social habit from scratch.

I translated those findings into requirements a team could design against: help people build a public listening identity, deepen connection with friends and artists, make personal reach usable, simplify sharing, and give people a community around music they already love. I also defined success up front so the work had a measurable bar: longer browsing, more new content consumed, more friends and followers, more artist subscribers, and word-of-mouth outside the app. Those metrics were hypotheses, not claimed results. We did not ship this into production, and we did not get to validate them with a second research pass.

ideation and conceptual design

Ideation started from behaviors we could already observe. People post Spotify links to stories so friends can see what they are playing; we asked why that moment had to leave the app, and Boombox became the in-product version of that story. Video platforms already solve “watch together” when people are apart; Best Buds applies the same pattern to a shared listen. A profile that only lists playlists still hides taste; Anthems makes identity scannable by pinning the songs you will stand behind.

Each concept had to survive a simple test: does it help a college-aged listener share or discover music without creating a second social network to maintain? That constraint kept the system to three features instead of a grab bag of social widgets. I prototyped the flows in Adobe XD so we could walk the journey from a public story, to a joined session, to a profile that explains the person behind the queue.

reflection

This was my first time working with a partner on a design project in a non-academic setting and under the short D&AD timeline. I learned how to run collaborative brainstorming sessions, give and take honest critique, and make scope decisions with a fixed design window. We planned to test the prototype with the same 82-person survey group, but we did not have time for user testing and consequent design iterations. Before we ever reached that test, we had already learned that listening was already social; we just needed to make Spotify the place it happens.
