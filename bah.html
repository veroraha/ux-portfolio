Mission support application

A classified ops tool I designed and owned for USEUCOM

Skills: UI + interaction design, UX research, usability testing Tools: Adobe XD, HTML + CSS

overview

In my latest role as a UX designer and product owner at Booz Allen Hamilton, I led design on a 25-person interdisciplinary team to deliver a high-stakes application for the United States European Command.

As a product owner, I used my subject matter expertise about the mission set and user base to provide operational context to the development team and make decisions about app direction. As a designer, I implemented what users actually needed to do their jobs, not what other people asked for on their behalf.

I traveled internationally to see how users really interact with the application and to note pain points that video calls and emails can't capture. I compared mockups to development builds, flagged styling issues in pull requests, and used my technical knowledge to provide solutions, cutting down the time it took for developers to address design feedback. My unique skill set closed the gap between product decisions, my designs, and the final deliverable.

The application I worked on supports planning, operations, and crisis management for users with different workflows and definitions of success. When we moved to a cloud platform, I used that as an opportunity to redesign the product around user feedback.

I can't share details of this project, but I can walk through the parts of my role that best represent how I work.

Research and enablement

I talked to key stakeholders and real users to keep product design aligned with how the application was expected to provide mission support. I worked with senior leaders in small-group feedback sessions to gather input on direction and priorities. I led familiarization sessions with users to build an understanding of how the app should be used to meet mission requirements, and I hosted hands-on trainings so people could work through real tasks rather than sitting through a demo. I also ran one-on-one usability tests with brand new users to gauge whether the interface was intuitive enough to use without a specialist in the room. I created enablement assets like user guides and training videos so what was practiced in these sessions would sustain beyond the room.

Design process

Our team is a rapid-prototyping shop, so I did not spend much time wireframing - I dove straight into high-fidelity mockups and iterated continuously. I created hundreds of screens that mapped every interactive state, specifying workflows click-by-click so developers were not left to fill in the gaps. My mockups were intentional and treated as the source of truth. In my design specs, I defined the intended layout, hierarchy, and interaction, which left little room for interpretation in development. I also built a design system, including a style guide with a color palette and typography rules, and reusable components for buttons, toggles, form fields, modals, and in-app navigation, so the interface stayed consistent as new features were added.

Design challenges

I've mentioned the tool's planning, operations, and crisis management workflows, each with its own set of users, constraints, and success metrics. My designs cut interaction cost, made operational status scannable, and framed a strategic view for senior leaders.

In planning, the main friction point was data entry. Users had to build tables one row at a time and open a modal to edit and save each row. I replaced those clicks with import/export and auto-population using what the system already knew about the mission set so planners could finish the task faster. I mirrored source material in the interface to match the existing mental model and reduce input errors. I also split the planning section into distinct pages with clear status indicators so the shared workspace always reflected the most up-to-date information, and no required field went untouched.

In operations, the problem was situational awareness. It was not clear to users what had already happened, what was happening now, and what was coming next. The task progression visualization was a donut chart, which proved ineffective for at-a-glance operational progress, so I redesigned how real-world actions were tracked, using a linear progress stepper with icons to make status easy to scan and understand. I also introduced a persistent activity feed to surface important changes and keep the operational picture up-to-date.

In a crisis, senior leaders need to make quick decisions, so the goal was to create a single strategic dashboard to absorb information at a glance. The challenge here was information priority. Different users wanted different things from the same tool, so I needed to decide what earned a place on the strategic view and what would only slow people down.

reflection

Working a dual UX designer + product owner role taught me what it costs to say yes. Newcomers, regular users, and decision-makers all depended on the same tool in different ways, so I considered changes not only by what one group gained, but how it affected the mission for everyone else. Now when I bring a design to a product owner, I bring the tradeoff with it.
